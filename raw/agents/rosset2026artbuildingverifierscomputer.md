## Problem

"progress in training and evaluating these systems is bottlenecked by a
deceptively difficult question: did the agent actually succeed?" And the stakes:
"the consequences of getting it wrong compound, corrupting both benchmarks and
training data."

Why CUA verification is harder than text: "computer use trajectories are long,
visually rich, and ambiguous… a task may be partially completed; success may be
achieved through unexpected paths; and failures may be subtle, appearing only
transiently in a screenshot buried deep in a multi-step interaction."

Prior art bound, from AgentRewardBench: "no LLM-based judge exceeds 70%
precision", against human inter-annotator agreement of 89.3%.

## Method

A verifier is `V : (g, τ) → R`. The paper argues `R` must be richer than `{0,1}`:
a tuple `(r_proc, r_out, d)` — process score in [0,1], binary outcome, and a
diagnostic report localizing failures. Four principles:

1. **Non-overlapping rubric criteria.** Five named rubric pathologies and fixes:
   *phantom criteria* ("requirements never stated in the task… inflating the
   denominator"); *cascading errors* (non-independent criteria multiply one
   mistake); *separate generation from scoring* ("Generating the rubric and scoring
   it in a single LLM call leads the model to create criteria tailored to the
   agent's behavior"); *hallucination detection* (score the rubric twice, with and
   without screenshot evidence, "to surface discrepancies"); *conditional criteria*
   ("buy organic blueberries, or if unavailable, buy non-organic").
2. **Separate process from outcome.** `r_proc = Σ_{i∈A} earned_i / Σ_{i∈A} max_i`
   over applicable criteria; `r_out` is binary, "would a reasonable user consider
   the task done?". "An agent that … navigated to the correct product, and but was
   blocked by a login wall before it could add-to-cart would [get] full process
   credit."
3. **Controllable vs uncontrollable.** Uncontrollable, not penalized in process:
   "CAPTCHA, login walls without credentials", entity non-existence, out of stock,
   "no results matching all specified criteria". Controllable: intent mismatch,
   reasoning errors, hallucinations, "Insufficient effort: giving up after a single
   failed attempt", execution errors.
4. **Divide-and-conquer screenshot context.** Rather than dumping all frames
   (WebVoyager) or a global top-30–50 (WebJudge), "our design scores each
   screenshot against every rubric criterion to produce a relevance matrix
   `R ∈ R^{(T+1)×N}`, grouping the top-k most relevant **per criterion**". Stated
   invariant: "no relevant screenshot evidence can go undetected in the pipeline,
   specifically to not miss any hallucinations."

Algorithm 1 runs: rubric generation → multimodal relevance scoring → top-k
grouping → evidence analysis → conditional disambiguation → reality check →
multimodal rescoring → side-effect detection → outcome verification → failure
diagnosis over "an error taxonomy with 7 categories and 24 subcodes". "Steps 7–9
… can be run as multiple parallel instances, with process score determined by
median of rubric scores, and outcome by majority vote."

**CUAVerifierBench**: (a) *Internal* — 140 trajectories from WebTailBench,
generated by Fara-7B, labeled in-house for process and outcome; "used for all
ablation studies … and the auto-research experiments". (b) *Browserbase* — 106
trajectories from Fara-7B on Online-Mind2Web, "with 2× annotator overlap per
trajectory", annotators "first calibrated on 10 practice trajectories with gold
annotations", then a **two-stage** protocol: a *UV-blind* stage (task, unscored
rubric, trajectory only) and a *UV-informed* stage (shown the UV's verdict and
asked whether they agree). Process labels are "the median of the annotators'
continuous rubric scores, then binarized at a ≥ 0.8 threshold".

**Auto-research study**: the UV is "approximately 3,000 lines of code and 2,000
lines of prompts … all designed iteratively by a human expert (the first author)".
An agent (Claude Code v2.1.87 with Claude Opus 4.6 (1M context)) reruns the same
loop, either from `// TODO` placeholders or continuing the expert's best prompts,
under the rule: "maximize Cohen's κ without increasing FPR; any FPR-increasing
change is automatically rolled back."

- Backbone control: upgrading WebVoyager to GPT-5.2 cuts outcome FPR 0.45 → 0.10
  on Internal "but it also dramatically increases FNR (0.24→0.44)", κ 0.31 → 0.43.
  Conclusion: "UV's advantage stems from its screenshot scoring design, not merely
  from using a stronger model".
- Two-stage annotation: "outcome Cohen's κ rises from **0.39 to 0.63**, and outcome
  FNR drops from **0.62 to 0.35**, while FPR remains near zero (0.04). On process
  labels, FNR drops sharply from **0.32 to 0.09**. Only **16.6%** of annotator
  outcome judgements flipped after seeing the UV's reasoning, nearly all moving
  from success to failure."
- Inter-annotator agreement on the Browserbase split: outcome **0.53–0.57**,
  process **0.36–0.45**; UV scores 0.58 and 0.43, "indicating that the UV agrees
  with humans about as well as humans agree with each other".

- Auto-research: expert "iterated over 32 experiments across three weeks", reaching

## Failure modes

- `[observed]` **The headline "benchmarks are roughly 2× optimistic" claim rests on
  treating the UV as ground truth at a scale where it was never validated.** Table 3
  says outright "The UV is treated as the reference label", so WebVoyager's native
  74.6% versus UV's 37.9% is a *disagreement*, not a measured error rate. UV's own
  human-validated outcome κ is 0.58–0.64 on n = 106 and n = 140. Propagating a
  κ ≈ 0.6 judge across ~600-task benchmarks and attributing the entire gap to the
  native verifier is exactly the move the paper criticizes others for. The
  AgentRewardBench spot check (8 of 30 relabeled) is the only place a *human*
  disagreement is sampled, and n = 30 with no second annotator.

- `[observed]` **Near-zero FPR is the optimization constraint, not a discovery — and
  it biases every downstream conclusion in the same direction.** The stated
  objective is "maximize Cohen's κ without increasing FPR; any FPR-increasing change
  is automatically rolled back", applied to both the expert's and the agent's
  iteration. The resulting outcome FNR is 0.31–0.32: the UV misses about a third of
  genuine successes. A verifier tuned to never over-credit will systematically
  under-report success, which is precisely the signature that makes native
  benchmark verifiers look optimistic in Table 3. The two headline findings — "near
  zero FPR" and "benchmarks overstate success" — are not independent evidence;
  they are two views of the same tuning decision. Attacking this needs the
  Table 3 gap re-derived under a verifier tuned for balanced error.

- `[observed]` **The most impressive agreement number measures persuasion, not
  agreement.** Outcome κ 0.39 → 0.63 and process FNR 0.32 → 0.09 are the
  *UV-informed* stage, where annotators were shown the UV's verdict and reasoning
  and asked whether they agreed. "Only 16.6% of annotator outcome judgements
  flipped … nearly all moving from success to failure" — i.e. the UV talked
  annotators out of successes, in the direction its own objective favors. The
  paper's central claim is defended on the blind κ (0.58 vs IAA 0.53–0.57), a
  margin of one κ point on 106 trajectories, which is thin; reporting the informed
  numbers alongside invites reading the strong result as the validation.

- `[observed]` **Both human-labeled sets are trajectories from a single agent built
  by the same group.** Internal = 140 Fara-7B trajectories; Browserbase = 106
  Fara-7B trajectories. A verifier developed over 32 iterations against one agent's
  failure distribution has no evidence of transfer to a differently-shaped one. The
  at-scale study does add GPT-5 — and notably UV's agreement with native verifiers
  *drops* for GPT-5 on every benchmark (κ 0.42→0.27 on OM2W, 0.49→0.31 on
  WebTailBench), which is what one would expect if UV is less calibrated on
  stronger agents' trajectories. No human labels exist for the GPT-5 slice to
  adjudicate.

- `[observed]` **Principle 3 (separate generation from scoring) is not actually
  maintained.** The rubric is generated from the goal alone, but then: "Sometimes,
  the rubric is modified for e.g. updating conditional criteria, or adding new
  criteria for unsolicited side-effects", plus a "Reality Check" step that
  "Reconcile[s] rubric assumptions against screenshot evidence". So the rubric *is*
  edited using the trajectory, which is the leakage the principle exists to
  prevent — just deferred by a few pipeline stages. How often rubrics change post
  hoc, and whether the added criteria are agent-specific, is never measured.

- `[observed]` **The controllable/uncontrollable split is a hand-drawn list with an
  unresolvable boundary case in it.** "Login walls without credentials" is
  uncontrollable and earns full process credit; "Insufficient effort: giving up
  after a single failed attempt" is controllable and is penalized. A trajectory
  that hits a login wall and stops falls under both, and the same LLM decides
  which from the same screenshots. No agreement statistics are reported for this
  classification even though the entire process/outcome separation — the paper's
  second principle — turns on it.

- `[observed]` **Attribution across the four principles is explicitly anecdotal.**
  "Anecdotally, Figure 1 shows that the good rubric design alone accounted for
  roughly half of the Cohen's κ gains", and the abstract concedes "these gains stem
  from the cumulative effect of the design choices above". The only ablations
  reported vary the backbone LLM (Tables 11–12); there is no leave-one-out over the
  four principles. A reimplementer therefore cannot tell which of the ten pipeline
  steps carries the result — and step 4 (per-criterion screenshot scoring) is named
  as "our main contribution" without an isolating experiment against global top-k.

- `[observed]` **The internal set is both the development set and an evaluation
  set, and only the auto-research arm is guarded against leakage.** The 140-trajectory
  internal set "is used for all ablation studies (§6–6) and the auto-research
  experiments", while the expert ran 32 experiments scoring against it. "A separate
  compliance agent audits each iteration to prevent memorization of test examples
  into prompts" applies to the agent; no equivalent audit is described for the
  human's 5,000 lines. Consistent with mild overfitting, UV's κ is lower on the
  held-out Browserbase set on both dimensions (0.58/0.43 vs 0.64/0.59).

- `[observed]` **17% of trajectories are unterminated and their accounting is
  unstated.** Fara-7B on WebTailBench has 17.0% unterminated, GPT-5 on OM2W 7.2%.
  Whether these count as failures for the native verifier, for UV, or are dropped
  is never said — and 17% is larger than several of the native-vs-UV disagreements
  being interpreted. The AgentRewardBench check explicitly restricts to trajectories
  "that terminated within step budget", showing the authors know it matters.

- `[observed]` **Load-bearing constants with no sensitivity analysis.** Process
  labels are "binarized at a ≥ 0.8 threshold" — every process κ in the paper depends
  on that cut. Top-k per criterion has no stated `k`. The number of parallel
  instances feeding the median/majority-vote aggregation is unspecified. None are
  ablated.

- `[stated]` **Parts of the paper were produced by generative AI.** "we disclose
  that parts of this work were produced by generative AI, including but not limited
  to auto-research studies, results, analysis, and code. We performed our best
  effort to verify the results were not hallucinated." Best-effort verification is
  the same standard the paper argues is insufficient for agents.

## Relevance

This is the strongest single piece of evidence for open problem 3 in
`wiki/agents/open-problems.md` ("Measurement flatters the method"), and it moves
that entry from an inference across three papers to a measured claim: on
WebVoyager, native verifiers report 74.6% where a rubric-based verifier reports
37.9%, and the native-vs-UV false positive rate is above 20% on every
benchmark/agent pair tested. It also supplies the vocabulary the wiki entry lacks
— process vs outcome reward, controllable vs uncontrollable failure, phantom
criteria, cascading rubric errors.

Two caveats worth carrying into the wiki. The UV-as-reference-label design means
the 2× gap is a lower-confidence number than it reads, and the near-zero FPR is a
tuning constraint that pushes in the same direction — so this is evidence that
benchmark verifiers are *loose*, not a calibrated estimate of by how much. Pair
with [[fan2026agentprocessbenchdiagnosingsteplevelprocess]] (same problem, tool
trajectories, human step labels rather than a rubric judge) and
[[yuan2026verifiableprocessrewardsagentic]] (which shows an unreliable process
reward is worse than a sparse one — directly relevant, since this paper's process
score is intended as RL signal).
